## Mathematical expression

- **Read formulas as parts of sentences.** Relation signs such as `=`, `<`, and `\in` express predicates. Check the grammar of the whole sentence, including any display, and place commas and periods according to that grammar. A display ending a sentence needs terminal punctuation. Prefer a direct formulation such as “We have $x=y$”; retain “that” where the construction needs it, as in “It follows that $x=y$.” Do not apply a blanket rule deleting or adding “that” before formulas. (§§2.1–2.2, pp. 4–5.)
- **Use complete theorem names.** Write “the Brouwer fixed-point theorem” or “Brouwer's fixed-point theorem,” with the article or possessive appropriate to the construction. Avoid combining both. Preserve established capitalization and naming. (§2.3, p. 5.)
- **Keep inline mathematics compact.** Prefer `a/b` to a stacked fraction in running text where legible. Display tall fractions, matrices, or complicated exponents when they disrupt line spacing. Follow the journal's conventions and check the PDF rather than mechanically replacing every inline fraction. (§2.4, p. 5.)
- **Distinguish a mapping from its value.** Introduce $f:A\to B$ with its domain and codomain clear from the definition or context. State monotonicity or differentiability of $f$ or $x\mapsto f(x)$; $f(x)$ denotes a value at an argument. If the value is itself a mapping, explain the distinction. Resolve ambiguity without adding redundant domains to every later mention. (§2.5, pp. 5–6.)
- **Make quantifiers and their scope clear.** Prefer “for all,” “for every,” and “there exists” in ordinary prose. Use symbolic quantifiers where a genuinely nested formal statement is easier to read that way. Do not replace “for any” without checking its intended scope, or change the order of quantifiers. (§2.6, p. 6.)
- **Normally begin sentences with words.** A short referent such as “The vector $x$” often makes a sentence boundary clearer than a bare symbol. Do not add an empty transition or a redundant sentence merely to avoid a symbol at the start. (§2.7, pp. 6–7.)
- **State and maintain vector conventions.** Make row/column orientation clear where matrix multiplication matters and check every product's dimensions. A column-vector convention is a useful default when the manuscript has none; boldface depends on the field or journal. Row vectors remain legitimate, and an established consistent convention should be preserved. The source's assertion that all vectors must be columns is explicitly personal. Do not copy its dimensionally invalid $xAx^{\mathsf T}$ example for a column $x$ and square $A$. (§2.8, pp. 7–8.)
- **Introduce notation with the right construction.** Use “Let $c=f(0)$,” “Let $c$ denote $f(0)$,” or “Denote $f(0)$ by $c$.” Avoid “Denote $c=f(0)$” and “denote $c$ as ...”. In “$c$ denotes the value,” the symbol is the subject. (§2.9, p. 8.)
- **Size delimiters to their contents.** Parentheses, brackets, braces, absolute-value bars, and floor/ceiling signs must enclose tall contents legibly. `\left` and `\right` can help; manual `\big`, `\Big`, etc. may look better. Inspect nested and multiline expressions. (§2.10, pp. 8–9.)
- **Distinguish indexed operators from binary operators.** Write `\bigcup_{i\in I} A_i` for an indexed union and `A_1\cup A_2` for a binary union. The same distinction applies to sums, products, and joins: use the intended operator, index set, and limit placement. (§2.11, p. 9.)
- **Distinguish optimal values, optimizers, and order concepts.** A maximum is the largest objective value attained over the feasible set; a maximizer is a feasible argument attaining it. There may be multiple maximizers or no maximum. In a partially ordered set, a maximal element belongs to the set and has no strictly greater element in the set; a greatest element belongs to the set and is greater than or equal to every element under the stated order. A greatest element, if it exists, is unique. Apply the corresponding distinctions to minima, minimizers, minimal elements, and least elements. An infimum need not belong to the set. Preserve the author's full-definition requirement and the stated order; do not turn a supremum claim into an attainment claim. The source's “often unique” wording is sharpened here for partial orders. (§2.12, pp. 9–10; see also the standing instructions.)

## Grammar and wording

- **Avoid comma splices.** Independent clauses need a conjunction, semicolon, or full stop; a comma alone is insufficient. Formula clauses count too. “Then,” “thus,” “hence,” and “therefore” do not by themselves repair a splice. For example, replace “Let $c=2$, then $c>0$” with “Let $c=2$. Then $c>0$.” (§§3.1–3.2, 3.10, pp. 10–11, 14.)
- **Make logical relations explicit.** Prefer “if” for a conditional assumption where “when” could suggest timing or obscure the implication. Preserve distinctions between a sufficient condition, a necessary condition, and an equivalence. Mathematical “when” and theorem wording such as “Suppose ... . Then ... .” can be clear and grammatical; the source's stricter avoidance advice is a preference, not a universal ban. Use logical transitions only when they explain an actual dependency. (§§3.2–3.3, 3.10, pp. 10–11, 14.)
- **Give pronouns clear referents.** Check what “it,” “this,” “which,” and “they” refer to. If several assumptions or conclusions could be intended, name the relevant condition or conclusion. Impersonal constructions such as “It follows that ...” do not require an antecedent noun, but the inference still needs a clear basis. (§3.4, p. 11.)
- **Use formal forms in papers.** Normally spell out “do not,” “cannot,” and “let us” rather than contractions. Possessive apostrophes remain appropriate. “Cannot” is the default negative of “can”; do not mechanically combine a special construction such as “can not only ... but also ...”. (§§3.5–3.6, p. 12.)
- **Check articles in names.** Use the correct article and preposition for a university or journal in the actual sentence. Official names, abbreviations, titles, and institutional exceptions matter; do not assume that every name with or without “of” follows one rule. Verify uncertain names rather than inventing a form. (§3.7, pp. 12–13.)
- **Hyphenate compound modifiers where established usage or clarity requires it.** A modifier before a noun may need a hyphen, while its predicative form may not. Fixed compounds and disciplinary terms have exceptions. Preserve familiar terminology and do not introduce unnecessary compounds. (§3.8, p. 13.)
- **Keep punctuation style consistent.** Follow the journal or manuscript's English variety for capitalization after a colon and serial commas. Include a serial comma when it prevents ambiguity. The source's APA-style capitalization advice is not a rule shared by all styles. (§§3.9, 3.11, pp. 13–15.)
- **Use singular and plural nouns according to meaning.** In the usual research context, “research” and “work” are mass nouns; “notation” and “literature” normally refer collectively. Use plural forms only where distinct works, notation systems, or bodies of literature are actually meant. (§3.12, p. 15.)
- **Prefer concise enumeration.** “First,” “second,” and “third” usually suffice; “firstly” is not needed. (§3.13, p. 15.)
- **Use clear, inclusive agent references.** Singular “they/their” is acceptable; it takes the usual forms “they are” and “they have.” If several agents make the referent ambiguous, name the agent or recast the sentence. Preserve a manuscript's consistent and appropriate pronoun convention. (§3.14, p. 15.)
- **Make bounds and their objects explicit.** “A bound on $X$” is a useful default for a bounded quantity. Distinguish the quantity being bounded from a parameter indexing the claim. Established constructions such as “an upper bound for a set” are not automatically errors; the source's insistence on “on” is a wording preference. (§3.15, p. 16.)
- **Distinguish restatement from examples.** “I.e.” means “that is”; “e.g.” means “for example.” Written-out phrases often read more clearly. In the source's American-style convention, the abbreviation is followed by a comma; surrounding punctuation must also fit whether it introduces a clause or a phrase. Do not use “i.e.” to introduce merely one example. (§3.16, p. 16.)
- **Distinguish hyphens, dashes, and minus signs.** In LaTeX text, `-`, `--`, and `---` produce a hyphen, en dash, and em dash. Use math mode for a minus sign. Write `$n$-dimensional`, with the word-joining hyphen outside math mode, rather than `$n-$dimensional`. Follow established style for ranges and joint names. (§3.17, pp. 16–17.)
- **Protect the meaning of relative clauses.** A restrictive clause identifies which objects satisfy a property; a nonrestrictive clause adds information about already identified objects. Commas can therefore alter a mathematical claim. American-style “that” for restrictive clauses and “, which” for nonrestrictive clauses is a useful default, but restrictive “which” is legitimate in other styles. Check the intended set and scope before changing either wording or punctuation. The same scope issue arises with “who.” (§3.18, pp. 17–18.)

## Citations

- **Give each citation a purpose.** Use it for attribution, support for a claim or method, a relevant comparison or disagreement, or other material relevance to the question. Explain the connection rather than appending an unexplained list. Consolidate this with the existing focus on the closest literature and accurate, generous descriptions of prior work. (§4.1, pp. 18–19.)
- **Distinguish historical credit from reading guidance.** Cite original contributions accurately. If the reader needs an accessible explanation of an old result or one in another language, add a suitable modern treatment where useful. Age or language alone is not a reason to omit a relevant original source or avoid checking what it actually proves. (§4.2, p. 19.)
- **Locate the result being used.** For a specific result in a book or long reference, provide the theorem, proposition, section, or page and check that its assumptions support the present use. A general citation is enough only for a genuinely general treatment. Do not invent or guess a locator. (§4.3, p. 19.)
- **Make the grammatical referent clear.** If an author-year citation denotes the authors, use an appropriate verb and agreement. If it denotes the paper, make that explicit when needed, for example “The paper by ...”. Avoid an unclear switch between the authors and their article. (§4.4, p. 20.)
- **Use precise result references.** “Theorem 2 of Author (year)” is a useful author-year form. “Theorem 2 in [7]” or “in the paper by ...” can also be clear. The source's preference for “of” is stylistic; exact attribution and a verified locator matter more than a universal choice of preposition. Preserve the manuscript's citation commands and bibliography style. (§4.5, p. 20.)

## LaTeX and layout

- **Align relations with relations.** In derivations, align `=`, `\leq`, and similar relation signs. Indent an arithmetic continuation so a leading `+` is not visually presented as another relation. Check multiline punctuation and line breaks. (§5.1, p. 20.)
- **Keep equations inside the text width.** Prefer sensible line breaks or shorter expressions; introduce an auxiliary symbol only if it improves comprehension and has a clear referent. Do not shrink a formula indiscriminately or add disposable notation just to fit one line. Inspect the compiled page for overflow. (§5.2, p. 21.)
- **Handle abbreviation spacing deliberately.** An abbreviation's nonterminal period should not accidentally trigger sentence spacing. Use `Dr.~Wang` where a nonbreaking space is also desirable, or `Dr.\ Wang` where a line break is acceptable; punctuation or existing macros may already handle the spacing. Do not put `~` after every period or change normal sentence-ending spacing. (§5.3, p. 21.)
- **Use the existing equation-numbering policy.** The canonical [Equation Numbering](EconTheoryWriting_principles.md#equation-numbering) section requires numbering only equations actually cross-referenced in the active manuscript, including later text and appendices. Do not create a second, weaker rule here. Number theorem-like items consistently with the manuscript template; do not impose manuscript numbering on informal notes or slides. `refcheck` can help diagnose unused labels and references, but is optional and requires contextual review. (§5.4, p. 21.)
- **Place footnote markers consistently.** The source places a marker after an adjacent comma or period; follow the journal's applicable convention. This is a placement rule, not a reason to move necessary definitions into footnotes or add optional detail. (§5.5, p. 21.)
- **Use appropriate mathematical ellipses.** With the relevant math packages, `\dots` often selects suitable spacing and height; use `\ldots` or `\cdots` where their position is deliberately needed. Inspect lists, sums, and matrix patterns rather than substituting blindly. (§5.6, p. 22.)
- **Check parenthetical spacing in prose.** Normally leave a space before an opening parenthesis and after its closing parenthesis when prose continues. Do not add spaces just inside the parentheses, before following punctuation, or within mathematical function notation. (§5.7, p. 22.)
- **Preserve author formatting preferences.** In particular, retain the [LaTeX pagination preference](EconTheoryWriting_principles.md#latex-pagination) against `samepage` wrappers. Readability fixes should use the manuscript's normal layout and figure conventions.

## Final review before delivery

Apply these checks to the affected text in context; use the full manuscript when a definition, citation, label, or notation change has wider consequences. This review complements the author's required source comparison, word-level diff, compilation, and rendered-page inspection.

1. **Meaning:** Are definitions complete, functions distinct from values, optimizers distinct from optimal values, and order concepts accurate? Are domains, quantifier scope, inequality strength, attainment, and matrix dimensions preserved?
2. **Mathematical sentences:** Read the prose and formulas together. Check sentence grammar, display punctuation, theorem names, notation introductions, indexed operators, and clear sentence boundaries.
3. **English and logic:** Check comma splices, inference words, conditional meaning, pronoun referents, relative-clause scope, contractions, and article or noun-number errors. Resolve ambiguity without adding repeated explanations.
4. **References:** Can the reader tell why each citation appears and find the exact result used? Verify authors, locators, assumptions, and the description of prior work; distinguish attribution from further-reading advice.
5. **Rendered layout:** Check equation width and alignment, inline height, delimiter sizes, ellipses, abbreviation spacing, hyphens versus minus signs, parenthetical spacing, and footnote markers. Audit equation references under the existing numbering policy.
6. **Proportionate editing:** Confirm that every replacement corrects an error or improves clarity in context, and that a source preference has not displaced author wording, mathematical scope, journal conventions, or the paper's economic argument. Report any unresolved ambiguity rather than silently deciding the author's intended claim.
