## Core Question

Every theory paper should be able to answer:

- What question is the paper trying to answer?
- Why is this question important?
- What is the contribution?
- How is the paper answering the question?
- How is it different from previous work?
- What are the main findings?
- What are the implications of the findings?

Write short answers to these questions before drafting the introduction.

## Types Of Theory Contributions

A theory paper's contribution usually comes from one or more of these:

- Asking a new question.
- Introducing a new model.
- Connecting a model to an important application.
- Identifying a new economic force.
- Developing new empirical predictions.
- Making a technical contribution, especially on a long-standing problem.

For theory writing, technical tools should be treated as means, not the end. The paper should ultimately say something economically meaningful.

## Introduction Structure

A canonical introduction should proceed roughly as follows:

- Broad motivation: explain why the area, question, or application matters.
- Contribution: state explicitly what the paper contributes. A useful discipline is to write the sentence "The contribution of this paper is..."
- Model: explain the core setup informally, without unnecessary formalism.
- Results: explain the main insights, driving forces, implications, and predictions.
- Related work: explain how the paper differs from and adds to the closest literature.

The introduction is not a mystery story. Readers should not have to infer the contribution or wait for a surprise.

## Introduction Style Rules

- Find a narrative and center the paper around one or two key results.
- Know the purpose of every paragraph.
- Make the first sentence of each paragraph identify the paragraph's role.
- Write the introduction modularly, often first in bullet points.
- Serve a dual audience: some readers only read the introduction; experts will inspect technical details and literature positioning.
- Do not leave it to readers to decide what the results mean.
- Do not make loose, grand, or unverified claims.
- Do not over-defend assumptions in the introduction.
- Do not spend much space on extensions in the introduction.
- Do not repeat the same motivation or citation too many times.
- Keep one explanation or intuition consistent throughout the paper.
- Be generous when discussing prior work; frame the paper as developing or extending the literature, not as attacking it.

## Literature Section

- The literature section should explain the relation between the paper's results and related work.
- It should not be a general survey.
- Focus on the closest and most important papers.
- Describe prior papers enough for readers to understand the contrast.
- Use footnotes for less central references.
- Remember that cited authors may be referees.

## Model Section

The model section should simplify how readers think about the problem.

Default rule: one paper, one model.

For a game-theoretic model, state succinctly:

- Who are the players?
- What can they do?
- What do they know?
- What are their preferences?

Avoid delaying these definitions, changing the order, or mixing them with too much interpretation. Short motivation for assumptions can stay in the model section; longer defenses should be delayed to discussion, extensions, or appendix.

Do not specify an overly general model and then introduce crucial assumptions only right before theorem statements. The reader should know what model they are studying.

## Analysis And Theorems

The analysis should tell the reader where it is going and why each step matters.

For each result:

- Before the result: define needed objects, remind the reader of relevant facts, and say in words what the result will show.
- Theorem statement: make it self-contained, rigorous, and as readable as possible.
- Proof: include it in the main text only if it is short, interesting, or central. Put standard or repetitive proofs in the appendix.
- Intuition: explain every important result in simple economic language.
- Implications: say how the result will be used, what it predicts, or why it matters.

Good results usually say what must happen, not merely what may happen, unless the possibility itself is surprising.

## Theorem Writing Rules

- Avoid long theorem statements.
- Avoid long equations inside theorem statements when possible.
- Use definitions before the theorem to keep the statement clean.
- A theorem should ideally read like a precise takeaway message.
- A useful formula is: define `p`, define `q`, then state "Every `p` is `q`."
- Lemmas can be more mathematical; main theorems should be more interpretable.

## Notation, Footnotes, And Appendix

- Eliminate all nonessential notation.
- Footnotes are for optional clarifications, not necessary definitions.
- A paper should generally have fewer footnotes than pages.
- Use the appendix for heavy notation, repetitive arguments, and technical details.
- The appendix should still serve the paper's narrative; it is not a dumping ground.

## Length And Scope

- Keep the paper as short as possible.
- Try to reach the main result by around page 15.
- Do not include every implication produced by the model.
- Include an extension only if it supports the paper's main message.
- If there are many theorems, decide which few the reader should remember.

## Default Instruction For Future Codex Work

When helping write an economics theory paper:

- Start by identifying the paper's contribution category.
- Draft or revise the introduction around a clear narrative, not a literature dump.
- Keep model exposition in the order: players, actions, information, preferences.
- Before each theorem, add a short roadmap sentence.
- After each important theorem, add intuition and implications.
- Move heavy proofs, extensions, and robustness details to appendix unless they are central.
- Watch for loose claims, repeated citations, inconsistent intuition, and unnecessary notation.
